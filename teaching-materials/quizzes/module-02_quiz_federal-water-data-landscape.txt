1.  Which statement correctly describes what the NASA SWOT satellite provides for rivers?
... SWOT's radar interferometer measures water surface elevation, width and slope (and water area), aggregated to SWORD reaches and nodes. It does not measure discharge; SWOT discharge estimates are a separate product still in development.
-   Only NWM simulates streamflow from weather inputs, and only USGS gages report gage height above a local datum.
*a) Water surface elevation, width and slope, derived from radar returns; no measured discharge
b)  Discharge measured directly by radar, for every river reach on Earth, each day
c)  Gage height above a local reference point, at fixed river locations
d)  Streamflow simulated from weather forecasts and land-surface physics


2.  Why is USGS discharge generally less certain than USGS gage height, especially at very high flows?
... The sensor records gage height every 15 minutes. Discharge is calculated from it with a rating curve, which is calibrated with periodic field measurements of discharge. At very high flows there may be few field measurements, so the rating is extended beyond them.
-   Discharge is not a daily mean of gage height, and it is not taken from the NWM; it comes from the site's own rating curve.
a)  Discharge sensors are less precise than gage height sensors in deep water.
*b) Discharge is computed from gage height with a rating curve, and field measurements at very high flows are scarce.
c)  Discharge is the daily mean of gage height, so short peaks are smoothed out.
d)  Discharge comes from the NWM and is assimilated into the gage record.


3.  You download NWM streamflow for the same reach and the same valid time from two model versions (v2.1 and v3.0), and the numbers differ. What is the best explanation?
... NWM values are model output, not measurements. A new model version can change physics, calibration and forcing, which changes the estimates for the same reach and time. Record the model version and configuration with any NWM data, and treat a series that spans a version change as non-homogeneous.
-   NWM output has no approval status and no per-value quality flag; those belong to USGS and SWOT.
a)  One of the values is still provisional and will be revised to match the other.
*b) Each model version can produce different estimates; NWM values are model output.
c)  The NWM quality flag for that value changed between the two downloads.
d)  The gage the reach is linked to was recalibrated between the two versions.


4.  Which statements about data quality information are correct? Select all that apply.

5.  At the same time on the same river, an NWM reach shows 1,000 m³/s and the nearby USGS gage shows 35,000 ft³/s. What is the best first interpretation?
... NWM streamflow and USGS discharge are the same quantity in different units: 1 m³/s ≈ 35.31 ft³/s, so 1,000 m³/s is about 35,300 ft³/s. Convert units before comparing products.
-   A factor of about 35 between the numbers is a unit difference, not a model error.
a)  The NWM is about 35 times too low at this reach.
b)  The two products measure different quantities, so they can't be compared.
*c) They nearly agree: 1,000 m³/s is about 35,300 ft³/s.
d)  The USGS gage is about 35 times too high during this period.


6.  A SWOT reach reports a water surface elevation of 120 m, while the USGS gage in the same reach reads a gage height of 20 ft at about the same time. What does this tell you?
... SWOT water surface elevation is in meters above a global geoid, while USGS gage height is in feet above a local gage datum. The two can describe the same water surface. Compare changes over time, or convert between vertical references, rather than comparing raw values.
-   Converting 20 ft to about 6 m does not fix the comparison, because the two numbers are measured from different reference points.
a)  SWOT is about 100 m too high at this reach and the values should be discarded.
b)  The values should match once you convert 20 ft to meters.
*c) Nothing yet: the two use different vertical references, so raw values don't compare.
d)  SWOT reports water depth here, while USGS reports elevation above sea level.


7.  You are lining up a SWOT reach and an NWM reach for the same stretch of river. What should you expect?
... A SWOT reach (about 10 km) comes from the SWOT River Database (SWORD), and an NWM reach is an NHDPlus segment with a COMID, often much shorter. The two come from separate river networks, so they don't match one to one.
-   The word "reach" is shared, but the networks, lengths and identifiers are not.
a)  The two reaches share the same identifier, so they line up exactly.
b)  SWOT reaches are defined from the NHDPlus network, so their ends line up.
c)  NWM reaches are usually longer, so each NWM reach holds several SWOT reaches.
*d) They come from separate networks of different lengths and don't match one to one.


8.  Which of these rivers is SWOT **least** likely to observe reliably?
... Plan on rivers at least 100 m wide, SWOT's stated requirement. Usable estimates are sometimes reported for rivers near 50 m, but that is not guaranteed. SWORD includes rivers about 30 m wide, but that is the river network, not what SWOT can measure.
-   Being in SWORD does not mean SWOT measures the river well: check the observations and flags for your reach.
*a) A 30 m wide headwater stream
b)  A 150 m wide river
c)  A 300 m wide river
d)  A 700 m wide river


9.  You need a value for every hour of a recent storm at one river location, which has a SWOT reach, an NWM reach and an active USGS gage. Which product **cannot** give you that on its own?
... SWOT observes a location only when it passes overhead: a few times per 21-day cycle, at irregular times. The NWM analysis is hourly and USGS continuous values are usually every 15 minutes.
-   SWOT's strength is spatial coverage, including ungaged rivers, not a continuous record at one point.
*a) NASA SWOT RiverSP
b)  NOAA NWM analysis and assimilation
c)  USGS continuous discharge
d)  USGS continuous gage height


10. You want to know how much water flowed at an ungaged reach during a 2010 flood. Which NWM output fits this question?
... The retrospective simulation is a single, multi-decade run over historical weather that covers every reach, which makes it the way to get a long record at an ungaged reach. It shows what current modeling can reproduce, not what was forecast at the time.
-   Archived forecasts show what the model predicted at the time, and operational forecasts only began in August 2016, so they can't cover 2010.
*a) The retrospective simulation
b)  Archived medium-range forecasts from that week
c)  The current short-range forecast from the NWM API
d)  The current analysis and assimilation configuration


11. You want someone to be able to repeat your downloads next year. Which records should you keep? Select all that apply.
... The correct options are: the SWOT collection short name (which includes the version letter) and access date; the NWM model version, configuration and reference time; and the USGS service, retrieval date and approval status. Products are reprocessed, models upgraded and provisional data revised, so the same query can return different numbers later.
-   USGS data have no version number in each row, and the products do change over time, so the variable name alone is not enough.
[*] For SWOT, the collection short name (with its version letter) and your access date
[*] For NWM, the model version, configuration and forecast reference time
[*] For USGS, the service you used, your retrieval date and the approval status
[ ] For USGS, the data version number printed in each row
[ ] Only the variable name, since these Federal products don't change

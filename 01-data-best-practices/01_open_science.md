# Open Science

"Open science" is the umbrella value system for data best practices, which at their core help facilitate collaborative science. There has been a shift in the wider scientific community towards seeing other scientists and disciplines as collaborators, rather than competitors. We all benefit from transparent and reproducible research, as it helps ensure the integrity and usability of scientific findings going into the future. This is especially true for water science and common complications water scientists have to navigate:  fragmented community, data-intensive problems, and funder mandates.

Open science exists on a spectrum made up of open data, open code, open access publications, and open methods/protocols. We will explore each of these concepts as we move forward through this training.

## Open science in practice

Open science is no longer an optional "nice to have" aspect of research, with many organizations requiring data best practices as part of the publication process. Some requirements from [NSFs Public Access Policy](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/document/faq-public-access#qa-group-1) include data management plans, machine-readable metadata, and for publications to be available free of charge. [AGU's Position Statement on Data](https://www.agu.org/science-policy/position-statements/open-data) emphasizes "Preserving Open Data as a World Heritage to Secure our Future", including topics like ensuring equitable access to trusted and reusable data, implementing metadata standards across disciplines and domains, and taking collective responsibility in maintaining data infrastructure.

[FAIR data principles](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/) are a very common way to measure how "open" science products are. We will go into more detail about FAIR in the Data Publishing section, but here is a quick overview:

- F: Findable - Metadata and data should be easy to find for both humans and computers
- A: Accessible - Once the user finds the required data, they need to know how they can be accessed
- I: Interoperable - The data usually need to be integrated with other data and interoperate with workflows for analysis, storage, and processing
- R: Reusable - Data should be well-described so they can be replicated or combined in different settings

It's important to note that "open" doesn't always mean completely accessible to everyone, all the time. It usually entails having clear and persistent ways to access the data, publication, code, etc. Sometimes, there are necessary restrictions around sharing different types of data, whether that's because of laws, ethics, or data sovereignty. [CARE principles for Indigenous Data Governance](https://www.gida-global.org/careprinciples) is a very important example. It is the people-centered counterpart to FAIR in regards to the necessity of data sovereignty and why it matters. 

- C: Collective benefit - data ecosystems shall be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from the data.
- A: Authority to Control - Indigenous Peoples’ rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognised and their authority to control such data be empowered.
- R: Responsibility - Those working with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how those data are used to support Indigenous Peoples’ self
determination and collective benefit
- E: Ethics - Indigenous Peoples’ rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle and across the data ecosystem.

Now that we have covered some of the reasons behind open science and adopting data best practices, we should cover some common barriers that come up in conversation. For more information on these barriers and others we aren't going to cover, we highly recommend taking a look at [The Turing Way](https://book.the-turing-way.org/reproducible-research/overview/overview-barriers/). 

Putting your code and data online can be revealing and intimidating. Sharing possible errors in your work is a real fear, but that is far outweighed by the feedback you may receive, and how you may help other scientists in their research. It is also a motivator to hold your work to a higher standard which benefits all who are involved and all who may learn from your work.

It takes additional time and effort, especially at the start of a project. Things like a testing framework, setting up version control, and deciding with collaborators which parts may be open source and how they can be shared. However, the time spent on these initial tasks saves you more time in the long run. Say a change is requested when you've submitted your work for review, having reproducible and version controlled data and code makes integrating that change much quicker.

We know it is easy to talk about open science, and more difficult to pull these practices into your own work. There is a wider push towards adopting these open science workflows, and here are some tangible ways to work towards them:

- **Opt for constructive peer code reviews during the development phase.** We are human and it is importance to foster an environment where everyone is comfortable sharing in-progress work for constructive feedback. It is often the best way to ensure quality and build confidence in your work.
- **Share work early and often with peers and collaborators.** This is related to our point about peer code reviews. In every phase of the scientific process, it is important to get other people's perspectives. They may think of an issue you've run into from another angle and help you find a solution that you may never have thought of.
- **Make sure you have dedicated and equitably compensated data staff.** This is geared towards supervisory and managerial positions but is important for us all to keep in mind. When looking at a project, it is important that all people involved know what their focus area is, that no one is juggling too many things, and that everyone is equitably compensated for the work that they are putting in. This helps boost morale and sense of community and ensures that everyone feels safe and confident in their contributions.
- **Publish data and code as their own citable products.** This makes your data, code, and findings accessible to the wider water science community. It may add an extra step to the process, but it will in turn lower the barrier of entry for someone that is building off of concepts related to your work. It also ensures that if someone does use your data or code, you will be properly credited. It is through collaboration that we can move the scientific community forward, together.
